Bigfoot 200 — the sub-100 plan

Race: Friday August 13, 2027, 12:00 PM · Marble Mountain Sno-Park → Randle, WA Course: 200.1 mi · 44,082 ft gain · 45,563 ft loss · 107-hour limit Goal: finish under 100:00 — the Western States qualifying standard Support: unsupported. Drop bags at every aid station, no crew, no pacers.

Source: every distance, gain, loss and cutoff below comes from the official Destination Trail runner manual. Segment miles sum to exactly 200.1, gains to 44,082, losses to 45,563, and the hour allowances to 107.0 — the chart is internally consistent.

Most of the internet still describes a ~208-mile version of this race with aid stations at Windy Ridge and Johnston Ridge. That course is retired. Ignore any plan built on it. The manual also warns that CalTopo measures the course short (184.5 mi) — the manual's mileages are the accurate ones, and they're what this plan uses.

Two known errors in the manual itself, already corrected here: the Quartz Ridge → Chain of Lakes row prints 2.6 mph where 16.2 mi ÷ 7.5 h = 2.16; and the prose for Start → Blue Lake says 2,980/2,490 ft where the chart says 3,280/2,743 (the chart figure is the one that sums correctly).

Read this first

Three things changed the moment the goal became sub-100 rather than "finish."

1. Sub-100 is not a fitness target. It is a stopped-time target. The gap between 107 and 100 hours is 6.5%. You will not find seven hours by running harder — at mile 150 there is no harder. You find it by not being stationary. The budget below allows 6 hours of sleep and 4 hours 12 minutes of aid across 12 stations — an average stop of 21 minutes, unsupported, doing your own resupply. A single two-hour sit-down at Chain of Lakes, which is exactly what happens to people, costs a third of your margin. The discipline that produces sub-100 is rehearsed in training or it does not exist on race day.

2. Unsupported is the expensive choice, and it costs roughly what you're trying to save. A crew hands you a prepared bottle and a plate and pushes you out. Unsupported you filter, sort a drop bag, refill, eat, and repack, alone, in the dark, at hour 84. Realistically that is two to four hours across the race versus a competent crew — most of your sub-100 margin. You also lose pacers, who are permitted from Elk Pass (mile 67.2) onward, through the two sections the race is known for losing people in, both of which you will now hit alone at night.

That is not a reason to abandon sub-100. It is a reason to be honest that finding a crew is worth more than any training block in this plan. If sub-100 genuinely matters, solving crew is the highest-leverage thing on this page. If unsupported is fixed, the plan below still works — it just spends its margin on self-sufficiency instead of speed.

3. Bighorn would be your first 100-mile finish, eight weeks before a 200. Your longest to date is Gorge Waterfalls 100K. The current plan goes 100K → nothing → first 100 at Bighorn in June → sub-100 at a 200 in August. That is two debuts stacked eight weeks apart, with the second one unsupported and four nights long. Bighorn stops being a gate and becomes an experiment, and an experiment is a bad thing to hang an A-race on.

Pencilled in: BURT 100, Bainbridge Island WA, Saturday February 6 2027. Close to home, in PNW winter — the condition you'll be training in anyway. It's a 54K loop run three times, which sounds monotonous and is exactly right: a loop means you pass a drop bag roughly every five hours, so it is the only race on the calendar where you can rehearse the aid-station discipline sub-100 depends on. Twelve timed transitions, unsupported, at least one at 3 AM. It sells out — confirm early.

BURT is not a Western States qualifier, and doesn't need to be. It earns the thing you actually need: a 100-mile finish and a full night alone, which turns Bighorn back into a gate you pass rather than a coin flip. Your qualifiers are Gorge Waterfalls 100K and Bighorn 100, both already in the plan.

The race plan — 97:00 schedule, 100:00 goal

The goal is sub-100. The schedule is built to 97:00, and the three-hour difference is not padding you hope to keep — it is the thing that makes sub-100 likely rather than a coin flip. You do not pace to the number you want to hit. You pace to a number that still gets you there after something goes wrong, because something will.

Two reserves, and they are different things

Reserve 1 — three hours of schedule slack. The gap between the 97:00 plan and the 100:00 goal. This absorbs the ordinary: a bad stomach hour, a wrong turn, a longer stop than you meant, weather. You spend it without deciding to.

Reserve 2 — two and a half hours of sleep at Twin Sisters. Deliberate, and spent on purpose.

Sleep is three naps: 1:45 at RD 9327 (mile 82, Saturday night), 1:45 at Chain of Lakes (mile 134, Sunday night), 2:30 at Twin Sisters (mile 171, Monday night). No sleep Friday night — you run the blast zone through the dark.

The weighting toward Twin Sisters is deliberate. It sits at mile 171 with 29 miles left, after both of the sections this race is known for, and the last 13 of those miles are road that has to be run at 3.8 mph. Most people arrive at mile 171 having spent their sleep early and then walk a finish they could have run. Sleeping there converts directly into time.

It is also your second lever. Cut it to a 45-minute nap and you claw back 1:45. Together the two reserves give you about five and a half hours against the 100:00 goal — but they are spent in order, and you should never touch the second one before mile 171.

The decision point is Chain of Lakes, mile 134, target 60:33:

  • On plan or ahead → run the schedule. Full sleep at Twin Sisters, run the finish.
  • Up to 2 hours down → still fine. That's Reserve 1 doing its job. Change nothing.
  • 3 to 4 hours down → cut Twin Sisters to 1:15 and keep moving.
  • More than 5 hours down → sub-100 is gone. Switch to finishing inside 107, which you will have ten hours of margin for, and enjoy the rest of it.

Do not buy sleep before mile 151. Extra sleep at RD 9327 or Chain of Lakes feels like the same hours, but it pushes Klickitat → Twin Sisters later, and that section has to happen in daylight. That's the one thing the schedule is shaped to protect.

What the clock puts where

  • The ropes and the knee-deep crossing arrive at dusk. They sit about 7.4 miles into the Blue Lake → Coldwater segment, roughly mile 19.6, around hour 7 — Friday evening, light going. Solo, on fixed ropes, into a river. Know the descriptions and have lights out before you need them.
  • The blast zone is a night crossing. 25.2 miles, no aid, last non-silty water at segment mile 6.2, and you're in it from Friday evening to 2:49 AM Saturday. The cutoff pace here is the most generous on the course (1.53 mph) because the race knows it's slow. Carry for ten hours.
  • Mt Margaret lands in daylight. Saturday morning, which is what the manual recommends and the best thing about this pacing.
  • Klickitat → Twin Sisters is almost entirely daylight — protect this above everything. Depart Klickitat 11:15 AM Monday, arrive Twin Sisters 10:47 PM. The notorious 19.4-mile blowdown section — faint trail, downed logs, bushwhacking, the section every single race report names — gets eleven and a half of its twelve hours in the light, and you are unsupported and alone in it. This is what Reserve 1 is really for.
  • Blue Lake is the tightest cutoff you'll ever be near. 42 minutes in hand at mile 12.2, off a 3,280 ft climb in afternoon heat. The only point on the course where the plan is close to a cutoff. Don't let a hot first climb become a race-ending one.
  • The last 13 miles are road, 385 ft of gain, and must be run. 3:23 for 13 miles is 3.8 mph at hour 94. The most trainable segment on the course, the place most people stop being able to run, and the reason Twin Sisters gets the biggest sleep.

What changes in training

The volume spine stays. What it's pointed at changes.

Weekly targets live on the Blocks page, not here. They were duplicated on both pages and the two copies drifted apart — this one still carried the pre-rescale ladder. One number, one home: Blocks owns hours, vertical, miles, peak day and night hours; this page owns race day.

Five things sub-100 adds that "finish" didn't

1. Night hours stop being a checkbox. You will be moving through four nights, alone, unsupported — roughly 40 hours of it. Whatever the cumulative target on the Blocks page, the shape matters more than the total: at least two efforts that cross a full night and continue into the next day, and one in Block 4 that crosses two nights. Hours banked on a lit path in one-hour pieces do not buy that.

2. Sleep restriction is a recurring session, not a Block 4 stunt. Two sessions on under four hours' sleep does not prepare you for 6 hours across 4 days. From Block 2 on, one long day per month starts on deliberately short sleep. In Blocks 4 and 5, practice the actual protocol: 2 hours down, alarm, up and moving inside 15 minutes. The skill is the waking up, not the sleeping.

3. Aid station discipline becomes a measured thing. 21 minutes average, unsupported, is a trained behavior. On every long day from Block 1: drop a bag at the car, and time the stop. Target under 10 minutes for a resupply, under 25 with a full change. Practice with cold hands. Practice at night. The failure mode is not one long stop — it's twelve average stops that each ran ten minutes over.

4. Descent volume gets its own prescription. 45,563 ft of loss, more than the gain. The descent HR gap monitor was already the right instrument; now it needs volume behind it. From Block 2: one descent-focused session per week, on rock, late in the day, on tired legs. Not loam, not fresh. The plan's original non-negotiable was right and under-resourced.

5. Late-race running speed becomes a training target. Beyond the closing road there are runnable stretches all through Lewis River, Chain of Lakes and Owens Creek. Everyone can hike at 2.3 mph on day one. Almost nobody can still run on day four, which is exactly where the seven hours live. Every long day from Block 2 ends with 30–60 minutes at target race pace on runnable ground. It will feel pointless and stupid at the end of an eight-hour day. That is the point.

Also, because unsupported

  • Carry weight is now a training variable. The 25.2-mile blast zone crossing with silty water means filtering plus a real carry. Do long days with race pack weight from Block 2, not Block 5.
  • Filtering is a rehearsed skill. Silty water, at night, tired. Practice with the actual filter.
  • Heat acclimation starts in Block 4, not Block 5. Noon start, blast zone, no shade, reports of 90°F, and no crew handing you ice. Six sessions was the plan when someone else was carrying your cooling. Make it twelve, starting in May.
  • Navigation is on you. Two sections of this course have "the trail becomes faint and disappears" in the official manual. Practice night navigation off-trail, solo.

Sub-28 with a controlled second half and disciplined aid says the sub-100 machinery works. Sub-30 with a four-hour death march says you'll finish Bigfoot in 104 hours — a fine day out, and not what you asked for.

The gate, restated

Three outcomes at Bighorn, decided in advance:

  • Under 28 hours, controlled, aid under 90 minutes → sub-100 is live. Run the plan.
  • 28–30 hours, or a finish that cost you two weeks → Bigfoot is on, sub-100 is not. Race it to finish inside 107, enjoy it, qualify another way.
  • DNF, or an injury finish → defer Bigfoot. There is another one next year and there is only one of you.

Write that down now, in September, while it's abstract and costs nothing. In June it will cost something.
